TOPIC Probability
📐 Range & Theoretical Probability
\[0 \leq P(E) \leq 1\]
\[P(E) = \frac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}\]

0 = impossible · 0.5 = even chance · 1 = certain

🔄 Complement Rule
\[P(A) + P(A') = 1\]
\[P(A) = 1 - P(A')\]

💡 Any "at least one" question → do "none" and subtract.

➕ Addition & Multiplication Rules
Addition rule — "A or B or both" \[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]
Special cases
If the events areThen
Mutually exclusive (cannot co-occur)\(P(A \cap B) = 0\) so \(P(A \cup B) = P(A)+P(B)\)
Independent (no influence)\(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)\)
Dependent (one affects the other)\(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)\)

⚠️ Mutually exclusive ≠ independent. Exclusive events never co-occur; independent ones often do, just without influencing each other.

🧪 Test independence from a two-way table: read \(P(A)\), \(P(B)\) and \(P(A \cap B)\) from the totals. Independent only if \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\) exactly (equivalently \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\)). Summer 2025: \(0.4 \neq 0.5 \times 0.7 = 0.35\) → not independent.

💡 Told the events are independent? Then \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A)P(B)\). Fall 2025: \(0.6 + 0.4 - 0.24 = 0.76\).

💡 "Exactly one of A, B" = \(P(A \cup B) - P(A \cap B)\).

🌿 Conditional Probability & Tree Diagrams
\[P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}, \qquad P(A) > 0\]
① Multiply ALONG a branch ② Add ACROSS branches

Branches leaving any one node always sum to 1 — use it to check the tree.

Law of total probability \[P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid A')P(A')\]

💡 This is just "add every path that ends in B" — it is how you get the denominator for Bayes.

🔁 Bayes' Theorem
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \times P(A)}{P(B)}\]
Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) ÷ Evidence
TermNameMeaning
\(P(A \mid B)\)PosteriorProbability of A given B
\(P(B \mid A)\)LikelihoodProbability of B given A
\(P(A)\)PriorInitial probability of A
\(P(B)\)EvidenceTotal probability of B

💡 Always build \(P(B)\) from both branches of the tree first: \(P(+) = P(D)P(+ \mid D) + P(D')P(+ \mid D')\).

⚠️ A rare condition plus an imperfect test gives a small \(P(D \mid +)\). Fall 2025: 2% prevalence, 90% detection, 5% false positives → \(0.018 / 0.067 \approx 0.27\).

Use when: the question reverses a condition — "given the outcome, what caused it?" (positive test → does the patient have the disease?)
UNIT 07 The Probability Distribution
🎲 Discrete Probability Distribution
\(X\)\(x_1\)\(x_2\)\(x_3\)…\(x_n\)
\(P(X=x)\)\(p_1\)\(p_2\)\(p_3\)…\(p_n\)
Two conditions that must hold
① \(p(x) \geq 0\) for every x ② \(\sum p(x) = 1\)
Mean = Expected Value \[\mu = E(x) = \sum x\,p(x)\]
Variance \[\text{Var}(x) = \sum x^2 p(x) - \big(E(x)\big)^2\]
Build the table, then the \(xp(x)\) and \(x^2p(x)\) columns. Variance = mean of squares − square of the mean.
Change of units — \(Y = aX + b\) \[E(Y) = aE(X) + b \qquad \text{Var}(Y) = a^2\,\text{Var}(X) \qquad \text{SD}(Y) = |a|\,\text{SD}(X)\]

💡 Summer 2025: bonus = 50 × deliveries − 50, so SD(bonus) = 50 × √1.2 = 54.77 Tk. Not in the slides — the table method always works, and this is a quick check.

⚠️ Read whether it asks for the variance or the standard deviation: SD = √Var.

🎯 Binomial / Bernoulli Distribution
\[X \sim \beta(n, p) \qquad P(X = r) = {}^{n}C_r \, p^r q^{\,n-r}\]
\(n\) = number of trials \(r = 0,1,2,\ldots,n\) \(p\) = P(success) \(q = 1-p\) = P(failure) \({}^{n}C_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\)
Mean \[\mu = E(x) = np\]
Variance \[\sigma^2 = npq\]
Three criteria (all must hold)
① Fixed number of trials ② Trials independent ③ p the same every trial

💡 Given mean & variance, recover the parameters: \(q = \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu}\), then \(p = 1-q\) and \(n = \frac{\mu}{p}\).

💡 "More than 1" / "fewer than k" → use the complement: \(P(X > 1) = 1 - P(0) - P(1)\).

⏱️ Poisson Distribution
\[X \sim P_0(\mu) \qquad P(X = r) = \frac{e^{-\mu} \times \mu^{\,r}}{r!}\]
\(\mu\) = mean events per interval \(r = 0,1,2,\ldots\) (no upper limit) \(e \approx 2.71828\)
Mean = Variance \[E(x) = \sigma^2 = \mu\]
Three conditions
① Events occur singly ② Constant average rate ③ Events independent

⏱️ Rescale first. Rate × interval = \(\mu\). Once every 48 h → in 60 h, \(\mu = \frac{60}{48} = 1.25\); in 24 h, \(\mu = 0.5\).

➕ Independent Poissons add: \(X \sim P_0(\mu_1)\), \(Y \sim P_0(\mu_2)\) ⇒ \(X+Y \sim P_0(\mu_1+\mu_2)\).

💡 \(P(X \geq 1) = 1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - e^{-\mu}\).

🔔 Normal (Gaussian) Distribution
\[X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\]
Probability density function \[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\; e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]
\(f(x)\) = probability density \(x\) = value of the variable \(\mu\) = mean \(\sigma\) = standard deviation \(\sigma^2\) = variance
Properties
ContinuousNot a count — a measurement on a scale
SymmetricHalf the values below the mean, half above
CentreMean = Median = Mode
Described byJust two values: \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\)
SupportNon-zero over the entire real line
📏 Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7)
IntervalContains
\(\mu \pm 1\sigma\)≈ 68% of values (34% each side)
\(\mu \pm 2\sigma\)≈ 95% of values (+13.5% each side)
\(\mu \pm 3\sigma\)≈ 99.7% of values (+2.35% each side)
Beyond \(3\sigma\) only about 0.15% remains in each tail.
UNIT 08 Binomial, Poisson & Normal Distribution
🎯 Binomial — Unit 08 notation
\[X \sim B(n,p) \quad P(X=x) = \tbinom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}\]

4 conditions: fixed n · two outcomes · constant p · independent trials.

⏱️ Poisson — Unit 08 notation
\[X \sim Po(\lambda) \quad P(X=x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\]

\(\mu = \lambda = \sigma^2\). Same as Unit 07's \(P_0(\mu)\) — just a different letter.

⚠️ Normal Notation Trap
\[X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2) \quad\text{— the 2nd number is the VARIANCE}\]

"Variance 16" → \(\sigma = 4\). "Variance 0.7" → \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.7} = 0.837\). Square-root first, then standardise. (Unit 07's handout wrote \(N(\mu, \sigma)\).)

📐 Standard Normal, z & Working Backwards
Forwards (x → probability) \[Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \qquad Z \sim N(0, 1^2)\]
Backwards (probability → x) \[x = \mu + z\sigma\]
You wantUse
\(P(Z < z)\)\(\Phi(z)\)
\(P(Z > z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\)
\(P(Z < -z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\) (symmetry)
\(P(a < Z < b)\)\(\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)\)
Inverse z values (area to the left)
0.75 → 0.674 0.90 → 1.282 0.95 → 1.645 0.975 → 1.960 0.99 → 2.326

💡 Two unknowns (μ and σ)? Each given probability gives one equation \(x = \mu + z\sigma\). Subtract the two equations to find σ, then substitute back for μ.

💡 "Expected number" = probability × total (e.g. 0.1587 × 468 ≈ 74).

📏 SD Levels & Six Sigma
\(\mu \pm 1\sigma\)68.27%\(\mu \pm 2\sigma\)95.45%\(\mu \pm 3\sigma\)99.73%
Within \(k\) standard deviations — any normal \[P(\mu - k\sigma < X < \mu + k\sigma) = P(-k < Z < k) = 2\Phi(k) - 1\]
k = 1 → 2(0.8413) − 1 = 0.6826 k = 2 → 2(0.9772) − 1 = 0.9544 k = 3 → 2(0.99865) − 1 = 0.9973

💡 The answer doesn't depend on μ or σ (Summer 2025 Q1d). Outside the band = both tails = \(2(1 - \Phi(k))\).

🏭 Six Sigma (slides): 99.99966% within \(\pm 6\sigma\) = 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO). Strictly, 3.4 DPMO assumes the industry's 1.5σ drift; a perfectly centred ±6σ is ≈ 0.002 DPMO.

UNIT 09 Continuous Probability Distribution
📈 Probability Density Function (pdf)
Three properties
① \(f(x) \geq 0\) for all x ② \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\) ③ \(P(a < X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)
Mean \[E(x) = \mu = \int x\,f(x)\,dx\]
Variance \[\sigma^2 = \int x^2 f(x)\,dx - [E(x)]^2\]

⚠️ \(P(X = c) = 0\) always, so \(<\) and \(\leq\) give the same answer. \(f(x)\) is a density, not a probability — it can exceed 1.

💡 Unknown constant \(k\)? Set \(\int f(x)\,dx = 1\) and solve. "Show it's a pdf" needs both \(f \geq 0\) and area = 1.

💡 Symmetric pdf → the mean is the centre of symmetry, no integration needed.

Same as Unit 07 discrete, with \(\sum\) → \(\int\) and \(p(x)\) → \(f(x)\,dx\).
📐 cdf, Median, Quartiles & Mode
Cumulative distribution function \[F(x) = P(X \leq x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt \qquad f(x) = F'(x)\]
\[F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < a \\ \int_a^x f(t)\,dt, & a \leq x \leq b \\ 1, & x > b \end{cases}\]
FindSolve
Median m\(F(m) = 0.5\)
Lower quartile\(F(Q_1) = 0.25\)
Upper quartile\(F(Q_3) = 0.75\)
Mode\(f'(x) = 0\), check \(f''(x) < 0\) and the endpoints
Probability\(P(c < X \leq d) = F(d) - F(c)\)

✅ Check \(F(a) = 0\) and \(F(b) = 1\). Median/quartile equations are usually quadratics — keep the root inside the range.

⚠️ Mode ≠ median. \(F(m) = 0.5\) finds the median only. For the mode, solve \(f'(x) = 0\) or check the endpoints. They only coincide when the pdf is symmetric: Fall 2025's \(kt(10 - t)\) gives both = 5, but Summer 2025's \(k(10 - x)\) has mode 0 and median 2.93.

💡 \(P(X > a) = 1 - F(a)\), and for a continuous variable \(P(X \geq a) = P(X > a)\). Summer 2025: \(P(X > 5) = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25\).

ESTIMATION Point & Interval Estimation
📍 Point Estimation — Good Estimator Properties
UnbiasedExpected value = true parameter
ConsistentMore data → more accurate
EfficientSmallest variance among unbiased estimators
SufficientUses all the relevant information in the data
Unbiased estimates from Σx, Σx² \[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} \qquad s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum x^2 - \frac{(\sum x)^2}{n}\right)\]
🎯 Confidence Intervals
Every interval — c = margin of error \[\text{estimate} - c \;<\; \text{parameter} \;<\; \text{estimate} + c\]
SituationIntervalMargin of error
Mean, σ known\(\bar{x} - c < \mu < \bar{x} + c\)\(c = z \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Mean, σ unknown, large n\(\bar{x} - c < \mu < \bar{x} + c\)\(c = z \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Proportion\(\hat{p} - c < p < \hat{p} + c\)\(c = z \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\), \(\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}\)
Finding z — named by the area to its left
90% → 5% each tail → \(z_{95\%} = 1.645\) 95% → 2.5% each tail → \(z_{97.5\%} = 1.960\) 98% → 1% each tail → \(z_{99\%} = 2.326\) 99% → 0.5% each tail → \(z_{99.5\%} = 2.576\)

💡 The table gives the area to the left of z, which still includes the left tail — you can't get rid of the initial 5% — so look up confidence + one tail.

Working backwards
Given the two limitsestimate = midpoint,  \(c = \frac{\text{upper} - \text{lower}}{2}\)
Given c, want σ\(\sigma = \frac{c\sqrt{n}}{z}\)
Given c, want z\(z = c \div \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Given z, want confidencetail = 100% − (area left of z); confidence = 100% − 2 × tail
Given a limit, want α\(c = \hat{p} - \text{lower limit}\) → z → confidence

💡 Claimed value inside the interval → consistent with the data. Outside → evidence against it.

💡 Higher confidence → bigger z → bigger c → wider interval. Bigger n → smaller c → narrower interval.

UNIT 10 Hypothesis Testing
⚖️ The Six Steps
① \(H_0\) & \(H_1\) ② Significance level α ③ Test statistic ④ p-value / rejection region ⑤ Decide on \(H_0\) ⑥ Conclude in context
Test statistic for a mean (σ known) \[z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\]
WordingH₁Tail5% critical z\(P(Z < z)\)
"less", "faster" (time), "reduced"\(\mu < \mu_0\)Left−1.6450.05
"more", "increased"\(\mu > \mu_0\)Right+1.6450.95 (z₉₅%)
"different", "changed"\(\mu \neq \mu_0\)Both±1.960.025 and 0.975 (z₉₇.₅%)

💡 The z-table gives \(P(Z < z)\), the area to the left. So a right tail of 5% means looking up 0.95, and two tails of 2.5% mean looking up 0.975. By symmetry, \(P(Z < -z) = 1 - P(Z < z)\), so a left tail of 5% is \(P(Z < -1.645) = 0.05\).

Decision
p-value < α  (z in rejection region)Reject \(H_0\) — sufficient evidence for \(H_1\)
p-value ≥ αDo not reject \(H_0\) — insufficient evidence
α 10%: 1.282 / 1.645 α 5%: 1.645 / 1.960 α 1%: 2.326 / 2.576 (one-tail / two-tail)

🎯 Where does the claim go? Whatever you want evidence for goes in \(H_1\) ("more than 80 hours", "greater than 60 wpm" → \(\mu >\)). \(H_0\) always contains the "=".

📏 σ unknown but \(n \geq 30\)? Use the sample's \(s\) in place of σ (Summer 2025, n = 40). With σ unknown and small n, a textbook would use a t-test — this course and its papers use z (Fall 2025, n = 16: both methods reject \(H_0\)).

✏️ "Sketch the critical region" (Fall 2025, 2 marks): a bell curve centred at 0, lines at ±1.96, both 2.5% tails shaded and labelled "rejection region", the middle labelled "acceptance region, 95%".

📝 Jeffrey: \(z = \frac{16 - 16.43}{0.8/\sqrt{15}} = -2.081\), p = 0.0188 < 0.05 → reject \(H_0\): the goggles helped.

⚠️ Never write "accept \(H_0\)" — write "insufficient evidence to reject \(H_0\)".

🚦 Type I & Type II Errors
H₀ is…TrueFalse
Rejected❌ Type I (false positive), P = α✅ Correct, P = 1 − β
Not rejected✅ Correct, P = 1 − α❌ Type II (false negative), P = β
Court analogy (H₀ = innocent): Type I = convicting an innocent person; Type II = letting a guilty one go.

✍️ "Define a Type I error in this context": write \(H_0\) first, then say "concluding [\(H_1\) in words] when actually [\(H_0\) in words]". Summer 2025 bulbs: concluding they last under 10,000 hours when they actually meet the standard — a good batch is wrongly rejected.

🎲 Binomial & Poisson Tests (Worksheet 02)
① X ~ B(n, p) or Po(λ) ② \(H_0\): old value · \(H_1\): the claim ③ \(P(X \leq \text{obs})\) or \(P(X \geq \text{obs})\) under \(H_0\) ④ Compare with α

⏱️ Longer period → scale λ first (7/month → 42 over 6 months). Large λ → \(N(\lambda, \lambda)\) with continuity correction: \(X \leq 32 \rightarrow X < 32.5\).

💡 Two-tailed binomial/Poisson test: compare each tail with α ÷ 2.

🔍 Spot the Distribution — Wording Trick
Ask three questions, in order
① A fixed number \(n\) of yes/no trials? → Binomial ② A count per hour / day / page, with no upper limit? → Poisson ③ A sample mean \(\bar{x}\) of measurements, with σ? → Normal (z)

💡 Units give it away. Continuous units (hours, kg, cm, words per minute) → normal. Whole-number counts → binomial if there's a maximum (n), Poisson if there isn't.

Example 1 — Binomial

A company claims that 30% of its customers prefer its new flavour. In a random sample of 15 customers, only 1 prefers the new flavour. Test at the 5% level of significance whether the proportion who prefer it is less than the company claims.

🔍 Fixed n = 15, and each customer either prefers it or doesn't → binomial, \(X \sim \beta(15,\, 0.3)\).

  1. \(H_0: p = 0.3 \qquad H_1: p < 0.3\) ("less than")
  2. \(\alpha = 0.05\), one-tailed (left)
  3. Test statistic: \(X = 1\), the number who prefer it, with \(X \sim \beta(15,\, 0.3)\) under \(H_0\)
  4. p-value \(= P(X \leq 1)\) \(= 0.7^{15} + {}^{15}C_1(0.3)(0.7)^{14}\) \(= 0.0047 + 0.0305\) \(= 0.0353\)
  5. \(0.0353 < 0.05\) → reject \(H_0\)
  6. There is sufficient evidence, at the 5% level, that fewer than 30% of customers prefer the new flavour.
Example 2 — Poisson

A help desk receives calls at an average rate of 3 per 10 minutes. After a new FAQ page goes online, only 4 calls are received in a randomly chosen 30-minute period. Test at the 5% level whether the rate of calls has decreased.

🔍 A count of calls over a time period, with no maximum → Poisson. Rescale first: 30 minutes is 3 × 10 minutes, so \(\lambda = 3 \times 3 = 9\).

  1. \(H_0: \lambda = 9 \qquad H_1: \lambda < 9\) (calls per 30 minutes, "decreased")
  2. \(\alpha = 0.05\), one-tailed (left)
  3. Test statistic: \(X = 4\) calls, with \(X \sim P_0(9)\) under \(H_0\)
  4. p-value \(= P(X \leq 4)\) \(= e^{-9}\left(1 + 9 + \frac{9^2}{2!} + \frac{9^3}{3!} + \frac{9^4}{4!}\right)\) \(= e^{-9} \times 445.375\) \(= 0.0550\)
  5. \(0.0550 > 0.05\) → insufficient evidence to reject \(H_0\)
  6. There is insufficient evidence, at the 5% level, that the call rate has decreased. Four calls could easily happen by chance at the old rate.
Example 3 — Normal (z-test)

The lifetime of a brand of battery has mean 50 hours and standard deviation 4 hours. A new design is tested on a random sample of 36 batteries, which last a mean of 51.5 hours. Test at the 5% level whether the new design has increased the mean lifetime.

🔍 A sample mean of a measurement (hours), with σ given → normal, z-test.

  1. \(H_0: \mu = 50 \qquad H_1: \mu > 50\) ("increased")
  2. \(\alpha = 0.05\), one-tailed (right); critical value \(z = 1.645\)
  3. Test statistic: \(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) \(= \dfrac{51.5 - 50}{4/\sqrt{36}}\) \(= \dfrac{1.5}{0.667} = 2.25\)
  4. p-value \(= P(Z > 2.25) = 1 - 0.9878 = 0.0122\). Equivalently, \(2.25 > 1.645\), so z falls in the rejection region.
  5. \(0.0122 < 0.05\) → reject \(H_0\)
  6. There is sufficient evidence, at the 5% level, that the new design increases the mean battery lifetime.
