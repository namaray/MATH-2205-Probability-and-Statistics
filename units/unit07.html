The Probability Distribution

1. The Probability Distribution

Definition: The probability distribution for a random variable describes how the probabilities are distributed over the values of the random variable.

In the development of the probability function for a discrete random variable, two conditions must be satisfied:

Non-negativity

\(p(x)\) must be non-negative for each value of the random variable — that is, \(p(x) \geq 0\).

Total probability = 1

The sum of the probabilities for each value of the random variable must equal one — that is, \(\sum p(x) = 1\).

The Distribution Table

\(X\) \(x_1\)\(x_2\)\(x_3\)— — —\(x_n\)
\(P(X = x)\) \(p_1\)\(p_2\)\(p_3\)— — —\(p_n\)

Mean and Variance of a Discrete Distribution

Mean = Expected Value
\[\mu = E(x) = \sum x\,p(x) = x_1p_1 + x_2p_2 + \cdots + x_np_n\]
Variance
\[\text{Var}(x) = \sum x^2 p(x) - \big(E(x)\big)^2\]
💡 Working Order

Always compute \(E(x)\) first, then build the \(x^2p(x)\) column. The variance is mean of the squares minus the square of the mean — the same structure as the short-cut variance formula from Unit 04.

2. Binomial / Bernoulli Distribution

Definition: A binomial distribution is one kind of discrete probability distribution that has two possible outcomes (success or failure / pass or fail).

Properties / Criteria

Binomial distributions must also meet the following three criteria:

🔢

Fixed Trials

The number of observations or trials is fixed

🔗

Independence

Trials are independent of one another

⚖️

Constant Probability

The probability of success (or pass) is exactly the same from one trial to another

When the random variable \(X\) satisfies these conditions we denote it by:

Notation
\[X \sim \beta(n, p)\]

The Binomial Probability Function

The random variable \(X\), which represents the number of successes in the \(n\) trials of this experiment, has a probability distribution given by:

Binomial Probability
\[P(X = r) = {}^{n}C_r \; p^r \, q^{\,n-r}\]
  • \(r = 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots, n\)  (\(n\) = number of trials)
  • \(p\) = probability of success
  • \(q = 1 - p\) = probability of failure
  • \({}^{n}C_r = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\) = number of ways to choose \(r\) successes from \(n\) trials

Mean (Expected Value) and Variance

If \(X \sim \beta(n, p)\), then:

Mean
\[\mu = E(x) = n \times p\]
Variance
\[\sigma^2 = npq\]
💡 Working Backwards

Given the mean and variance you can recover \(n\) and \(p\): since \(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\mu} = \dfrac{npq}{np} = q\), you get \(q\) directly, then \(p = 1 - q\) and \(n = \dfrac{\mu}{p}\). This is exactly what Exercise 3 below asks for.

3. Poisson Distribution

Definition: In probability theory and statistics, the Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, if these events occur with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event.

Conditions / Properties

1️⃣

Occurs Singly

Events happen one at a time, not in clumps

📊

Constant Rate

The average rate at which events occur is always the same

🔗

Independence

Events are independent of one another

The Poisson Probability Function

The random variable \(X\) satisfying a Poisson distribution, \(X \sim P_0(\mu)\), has the probability distribution given by:

Poisson Probability
\[P(X = r) = \frac{e^{-\mu} \times \mu^{\,r}}{r!}\]
  • \(\mu\) = the mean number of events in the interval considered
  • \(r = 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)  (there is no upper limit)
  • \(e \approx 2.71828\)

Mean (Expected Value) and Variance

Mean = Variance
\[E(x) = \sigma^2 = \mu\]
⚡ The Poisson Signature

For a Poisson distribution the mean and the variance are equal. If a question tells you the mean and the variance of a count are roughly the same, it is hinting at Poisson; if the variance is clearly smaller than the mean, it is hinting at binomial (since \(\sigma^2 = npq < np = \mu\)).

🕒 Scaling the Interval

The rate scales with the interval. If breakdowns occur once every 48 hours, then in 60 hours \(\mu = \frac{60}{48} = 1.25\), and in 24 hours \(\mu = 0.5\). Always rescale \(\mu\) to the interval the question asks about before substituting.

➕ Adding Poissons

If \(X \sim P_0(\mu_1)\) and \(Y \sim P_0(\mu_2)\) are independent, then \(X + Y \sim P_0(\mu_1 + \mu_2)\). Two independent sources of emissions combine by adding their means.

4. Normal Distribution

Definition: A normal distribution, or Gaussian distribution, is one of the continuous probability distributions.

In a normal distribution, data are symmetrically distributed with no skew. Most values cluster around a central region, with values tapering off as they go further away from the centre. The measures of central tendency (mean, mode and median) are the same in a normal distribution. In graphical form, the normal distribution appears as a "bell curve".

Properties of Normal Distributions

Normal distributions have key characteristics that are easy to spot in graphs:

  • The mean, median and mode are the same.
  • The distribution is symmetric about the mean — half the values fall below the mean and half above the mean — and is non-zero over the entire real line.
  • The distribution can be described by two values: the mean and the standard deviation.

If a variable \(X\) is normally distributed then it is symbolised as:

Notation
\[X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\]

The parameters use the Greek symbol \(\mu\) (mu) for the population mean and \(\sigma\) (sigma) for the population standard deviation.

The Normal Probability Density Function

Normal PDF
\[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \; e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\]
SymbolMeaning
\(f(x)\)Probability (density)
\(x\)Value of the variable
\(\mu\)Mean
\(\sigma\)Standard deviation
\(\sigma^2\)Variance

The Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7 Rule)

Empirical rule: The empirical rule, or the 68-95-99.7 rule, tells you where most of your values lie in a normal distribution.
📗

68%

Around 68% of values are within 1 standard deviation from the mean — \(\mu \pm \sigma\)

📘

95%

Around 95% of values are within 2 standard deviations from the mean — \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\)

📙

99.7%

Around 99.7% of values are within 3 standard deviations from the mean — \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\)

Splitting the Curve

Because the curve is symmetric, each band splits evenly either side of the mean: 34% + 34% inside \(\pm\sigma\), then 13.5% in each of the next bands, then 2.35% in each of the next, leaving about 0.15% in each tail beyond \(3\sigma\).

Why Do Normal Distributions Matter?

All kinds of variables in the natural and social sciences are normally or approximately normally distributed. Height, birth weight, reading ability, job satisfaction and SAT scores are just a few examples of such variables. Because normally distributed variables are so common, many statistical tests are designed for normally distributed populations.

5. Standardising: the z-Score

📎 Supplementary

This section is not in the Unit 07 handout, but you need it to actually compute probabilities from a normal distribution — the handout gives the curve and its properties, and the z-score is how you turn those into numbers with a standard normal table.

Definition: The standard normal distribution is the normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, written \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\). Any normal variable can be converted to it by standardising.
z-Score
\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]

The z-score says how many standard deviations the value \(x\) lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean.

Reading Probabilities
\[P(X < x) = P\!\left(Z < \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = \Phi(z)\]

Useful Identities

You wantUse
\(P(Z > z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\)
\(P(Z < -z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\)  (by symmetry)
\(P(a < Z < b)\)\(\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)\)
📝 Worked Example

Exam marks are \(X \sim N(60, 12)\). Find \(P(X < 78)\).

\[z = \frac{78 - 60}{12} = 1.5\]

From the standard normal table, \(\Phi(1.5) \approx 0.9332\), so about 93.3% of candidates score below 78.

Sanity Check

The empirical rule is just the z-table at round numbers: \(\Phi(1) \approx 0.84\), \(\Phi(2) \approx 0.977\), \(\Phi(3) \approx 0.9987\) — double the middle and you get 68%, 95% and 99.7%.

🎯 How to Spot It

If the question caps the count at a fixed number of trials ("out of 12 customers"), it is binomial. If the count has no upper limit and comes with a rate per unit of time or space ("6 per hour"), it is Poisson. If the quantity is a measurement that can take any value ("heights are normally distributed"), it is normal.
