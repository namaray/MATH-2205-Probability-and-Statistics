UNIT 10 · FINAL TERM

Hypothesis Testing

1. A Motivating Example

📝 Jeffrey's Goggles

Jeffrey, as an eight-year-old, established a mean time of 16.43 seconds for swimming the 25-yard freestyle, with a standard deviation of 0.8 seconds. His dad, Frank, thought that Jeffrey could swim the 25-yard freestyle faster using goggles. Frank bought Jeffrey a new pair of expensive goggles and timed Jeffrey for 15 25-yard freestyle swims. For the 15 swims, Jeffrey's mean time was 16 seconds. Frank thought that the goggles helped Jeffrey to swim faster than the 16.43 seconds.

16 seconds is faster than 16.43 — but swimmers vary from race to race anyway. Is 16 seconds so much faster that luck can't explain it? Hypothesis testing is the formal way to answer "is this difference real, or just chance?"

2. What is Hypothesis Testing?

Definition: Hypothesis testing is a statistical inference procedure that uses data from a sample to draw conclusions about a population parameter. That is, it is a systematic way to test claims or ideas about a group or population.

The Six Steps

Set the null and alternative hypotheses

\(H_0\) and \(H_1\)

Set the level of significance

\(\alpha\), usually 5%

Calculate the test statistic

e.g. \(z\)

Calculate the probability value (p-value), or find the rejection region

How surprising is the result if \(H_0\) is true?

Make a decision about the null hypothesis

Reject or do not reject \(H_0\)

State an overall conclusion

In the words of the original question

3. Step 1 — The Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Null hypothesis \(H_0\): A statement of a particular parameter value. It is assumed to be true until there is evidence to suggest otherwise.
Alternative hypothesis \(H_1\): The opposite of what the null hypothesis states — a range of alternative values in which the parameter may fall.

\(H_0\) is the "nothing has changed / no effect" position — like "innocent until proven guilty". \(H_1\) is the claim you're trying to find evidence for. You only abandon \(H_0\) if the data make it look unbelievable.

📝 Example

We want to test whether the mean GPA of students in UIU is different from 2.5 (out of 4.0). The null and alternative hypotheses are:

\[H_0: \mu = 2.5\] \[H_1: \mu < 2.5 \quad\text{or}\quad H_1: \mu > 2.5 \quad\text{or}\quad H_1: \mu \neq 2.5\]

— chosen based on the wording of the question. Here the word is "different", so \(H_1: \mu \neq 2.5\).

Choosing H₁ from the Wording

For Jeffrey, "faster" means a smaller time, so \(H_1: \mu < 16.43\).

4. Step 2 — The Level of Significance (α)

Level of significance: "How much risk of wrong rejection we are willing to accept."
  • If \(\alpha = 0.05\), we accept a 5% risk of wrongly rejecting the null hypothesis.
  • If \(\alpha = 0.01\), we are more strict and allow only a 1% risk.

\(\alpha\) is the bar for "surprising enough". At 5%, anything that would happen less than 1 time in 20 by pure chance counts as evidence against \(H_0\). A smaller \(\alpha\) is a stricter judge: you need stronger evidence before rejecting.

5. Steps 3 & 4 — Test Statistic and p-value

Test Statistic for a Mean (σ known)
\[z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}\]

\(\mu_0\) is the value in \(H_0\). The denominator \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is the standard error — the spread of sample means, which is smaller than the spread of individual values.

p-value: The probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming \(H_0\) is true.

6. Steps 5 & 6 — Decision and Conclusion: Jeffrey's Test in Full

📝 Did the goggles help?

Using the information in Section 1, test at the 5% level whether the goggles helped Jeffrey swim faster.

Show solution

Assume the goggles did nothing (mean still 16.43 s). How likely is an average of 16 s or less over 15 swims, just by luck? If that's under 5%, the goggles probably helped.

Step 1 — Hypotheses
\[H_0: \mu = 16.43 \qquad H_1: \mu < 16.43 \quad\text{(faster = smaller time)}\]
Step 2 — Significance level

\(\alpha = 0.05\), one-tailed (left). The slides don't state \(\alpha\); 5% is the standard choice.

Steps 3 & 4 — Test statistic and p-value
\[z = \frac{16 - 16.43}{0.8/\sqrt{15}} = \frac{-0.43}{0.2066} = -2.081\] \[\text{p-value} = P(Z < -2.081) = 1 - \Phi(2.081) = 1 - 0.9812 = 0.0188\]
Step 5 — Decision

\(0.0188 < 0.05\) (equivalently, \(z = -2.081 < -1.645\)), so there exists sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis.

Step 6 — Conclusion

The use of goggles has an effect: Jeffrey swims faster with them.

z = −2.081, p = 0.0188 < 0.05 → reject H₀ · the goggles helped
⚠️ Wording Matters

If you don't reject \(H_0\), write "there is insufficient evidence to reject \(H_0\)" — never "\(H_0\) is true". Not finding evidence isn't the same as proving there's no effect.

7. Type I and Type II Errors

Hypothesis tests are decisions made from sample data, so they can be wrong in two different ways:

Type I error (false positive): Rejecting the null hypothesis \(H_0\) even though it is actually true.
Type II error (false negative): Not rejecting the null hypothesis \(H_0\), even though it is false.

Think of a court case where \(H_0\) = "innocent". A Type I error is convicting an innocent person. A Type II error is letting a guilty person go free. \(\alpha\) is the risk of the first mistake, which is why we keep it small. Making \(\alpha\) smaller, though, makes the second mistake more likely.

For Jeffrey

A Type I error would be concluding the goggles help when really they make no difference. A Type II error would be concluding there's no evidence they help when really they do.

8. Beyond the z-test

The six steps work for any distribution, not just the normal. Part C of Worksheet 02 runs the same procedure with binomial and Poisson distributions. There, the p-value comes from \(P(X \leq \text{observed})\) rather than from a \(z\)-table.
