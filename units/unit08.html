UNIT 08 · FINAL TERM

Binomial, Poisson & Normal Distribution

How This Unit Relates to Unit 07

Unit 08 revisits the three distributions from Unit 07, then goes much deeper on the normal distribution: the standard normal \(Z\), converting with \(Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\), working backwards to find \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and Six Sigma. The binomial and Poisson parts are a short refresher; the normal part is where the new material is.

1. Binomial Distribution

The slides open with two questions that need a new tool to answer:

  • What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in 5 coin tosses?
  • What is the probability of getting at least 7 correct answers out of 10 multiple-choice questions?
Definition: The binomial distribution is a discrete probability distribution for the number of successes in a fixed number of independent yes/no trials.

Conditions of a Binomial Experiment

Fixed number of trials (n)

The experiment is repeated a set number of times.

Binary outcomes

Each trial has only two possible outcomes: success (S) or failure (F).

Constant probability (p)

The probability of success is the same for each trial.

Independence

Each trial is independent of the others.

Binomial Probability Formula

Notation
\[X \sim B(n, p)\]
Probability
\[P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}\, p^x (1 - p)^{n - x}, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n\] \[\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!} \quad\text{(the binomial coefficient, same as } {}^nC_x\text{)}\]

Mean \(= np\) and variance \(= np(1-p)\), as in Unit 07.

Answering the Opening Questions

📝 Exactly 3 heads in 5 tosses

\(X \sim B(5, 0.5)\):

\[P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3}(0.5)^3(0.5)^2 = 10 \times \frac{1}{32} = 0.3125\]
📝 At least 7 out of 10 multiple-choice questions

The slide doesn't say how many options each question has. Assuming 4 options and pure guessing, \(p = 0.25\), so \(X \sim B(10, 0.25)\):

\[P(X \geq 7) = P(7) + P(8) + P(9) + P(10) = 0.0035\]

About a 1 in 285 chance — guessing your way to 70% is very unlikely.

Example 1 & Example 2

📝 Example 1

A driving test is passed by 70% of people at their attempt. Find the probability that (i) exactly 5 people out of 10 will pass, (ii) more than 1 person out of 8 will pass.

Show solution

Each person is one trial that either passes (0.7) or fails (0.3). For "more than 1", it is quicker to subtract the two small cases (0 and 1) from 1 than to add up seven big ones.

(i) n = 10, p = 0.7
\[P(X = 5) = \binom{10}{5}(0.7)^5(0.3)^5 = 252 \times 0.16807 \times 0.00243 = 0.1029\]
(ii) n = 8, p = 0.7
\[P(X > 1) = 1 - P(0) - P(1) = 1 - (0.3)^8 - 8(0.7)(0.3)^7 = 1 - 0.00007 - 0.00122 = 0.9987\] (i) 0.1029 · (ii) 0.9987
📝 Example 2

65% of all watches sold by a shop have a digital display and 35% have an analog display. Find the probability that, out of the next 12 customers who buy a watch, fewer than 10 choose one with a digital display. [4]

Show solution

"Fewer than 10" means 0 up to 9 — ten cases. The opposite, "10, 11 or 12", is only three cases, so work out those and subtract from 1.

\[X \sim B(12, 0.65)\] \[P(X < 10) = 1 - \big[P(10) + P(11) + P(12)\big] = 1 - (0.1088 + 0.0368 + 0.0057) = 0.8487\] 0.849

Both examples are also fully worked in Unit 07, where they first appeared.

2. Poisson Distribution

Definition: Like the binomial distribution, the Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution. It expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, provided the events occur with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event.

Conditions for a Poisson Process

Single & independent

Events occur singly and independently.

Constant rate

The average rate (mean number of occurrences) \(\lambda\) is constant.

No simultaneous events

Two events cannot occur at the exact same instant — occurrences are discrete.

Notation & Probability
\[X \sim Po(\lambda) \qquad P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\]
Mean and Variance
\[E(x) = \mu = \lambda \qquad \text{var}(x) = \sigma^2 = \lambda \qquad \mu = \lambda = \sigma^2\]
Same Thing, Different Letter

Unit 07 writes the Poisson mean as \(\mu\) and the notation as \(P_0(\mu)\). Unit 08 writes it as \(\lambda\) and \(Po(\lambda)\). They mean exactly the same thing.

3. The Normal Distribution

Measure the heights of everyone in a room. They won't all be the same — but most people will be somewhere near the average, and very tall or very short people will be rare. Plot that and you get the bell-shaped normal distribution. The same pattern appears everywhere in nature, education, business and science.

Definition: A normal distribution is a continuous probability distribution that is symmetric about the mean. It describes how the values of a variable are distributed.

Properties

  • The graph is symmetric and bell-shaped.
  • The peak is at the mean \(\mu\).
  • All three measures of central tendency are equal and at the centre: Mean = Mode = Median.
  • Total area under the curve = 1.
  • The tails approach, but never touch, the horizontal axis.
  • Extreme values are possible, but unlikely.
Notation
\[X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\]
⚠️ Notation Trap — Read the Second Number Carefully

Unit 08 writes \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\): the second number is the variance. Unit 07's handout wrote \(N(\mu, \sigma)\) with the standard deviation. Exam questions usually say the word: "variance 16" means \(\sigma = \sqrt{16} = 4\), and "variance 0.7" means \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.7} \approx 0.837\). Always take the square root before standardising.

Area = Probability

The total area under the curve is 1, representing the entire probability space. So the area under the curve over any interval is exactly the probability that a randomly selected value falls in that interval.

📝 Example

The scores of a mathematics exam are normally distributed with a mean of 70 and a standard deviation of 10, i.e. \(X \sim N(70, 10^2)\). What is the probability that a randomly selected student scored more than 85?

Show solution

85 is 15 marks above the average, which is 1.5 standard deviations. Look up how much area lies beyond 1.5 standard deviations — that is the fraction of students scoring above 85.

\[P(X > 85) = P\left(Z > \frac{85 - 70}{10}\right) = P(Z > 1.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668\] P(X > 85) ≈ 0.0668 — about 6.7% of students

4. The Standard Normal Distribution (Z)

Definition: A normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1 is known as the standard normal distribution, or \(Z\) distribution.
Standard Normal
\[Z \sim N(0, 1^2)\]

What μ and σ Do to the Curve

Converting Any Normal to Z

Every normal curve is the same shape, just shifted and stretched. Subtracting \(\mu\) slides it to centre on 0; dividing by \(\sigma\) squeezes it to width 1. After that, one table — the \(Z\)-table — works for every normal distribution.

Conversion
\[X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2) \;\longrightarrow\; Z \sim N(0, 1^2) \qquad Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\]

Reading the Z-table

The table gives \(\Phi(z) = P(Z < z)\), the area to the left of \(z\). Everything else comes from that one number:

You wantUseWhy
\(P(Z < z)\)\(\Phi(z)\)Read straight from the table
\(P(Z > z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\)Total area is 1
\(P(Z < -z)\)\(1 - \Phi(z)\)The curve is symmetric
\(P(a < Z < b)\)\(\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)\)Big left area minus small left area

Working Backwards (Inverse Normal)

When the question gives you a probability and asks for a value, run the conversion in reverse:

Inverse
\[\text{probability} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Phi^{-1}\;}\; z \;\longrightarrow\; x = \mu + z\sigma\]

5. Worked Examples

Example 01 — Bolt Lengths

📝 Example 01

The length of a bolt produced by a machine is normally distributed with mean 18.5 cm and variance 0.7 cm². Find the probability that a randomly selected bolt is less than 18.85 cm long.

Show solution

The question gives the variance, so take its square root first to get \(\sigma\). Then 18.85 is only 0.35 cm above the mean — less than half a standard deviation — so the answer should be a bit more than one half.

\[\sigma = \sqrt{0.7} = 0.8367\] \[P(X < 18.85) = P\left(Z < \frac{18.85 - 18.5}{0.8367}\right) = P(Z < 0.418) = 0.6621\] ≈ 0.662

Example 02 — Clinic Waiting Times

📝 Example 02

The waiting times, in minutes, for patients at a clinic are normally distributed with mean 13 and variance 16.

  • (a) Calculate the probability that a randomly selected patient has to wait for more than 16.5 minutes.
  • (b) Last month 468 patients attended the clinic. Calculate an estimate of the number who waited for less than 9 minutes.
Show solution

Variance 16 means \(\sigma = 4\). For (b), first find what fraction of patients wait under 9 minutes, then multiply by the number of patients — just like "expected number" in the probability chapter.

(a) More than 16.5 minutes
\[P(X > 16.5) = P\left(Z > \frac{16.5 - 13}{4}\right) = P(Z > 0.875) = 1 - 0.8092 = 0.1908\]
(b) Fewer than 9 minutes, out of 468
\[P(X < 9) = P\left(Z < \frac{9 - 13}{4}\right) = P(Z < -1) = 1 - \Phi(1) = 0.1587\] \[468 \times 0.1587 = 74.3 \approx 74 \text{ patients}\] (a) 0.191 · (b) about 74 patients

Example 03 — Tomatoes, and Finding μ

📝 Example 03

Tomatoes from a certain producer have masses which are normally distributed with mean 90 grams and standard deviation 17.7 grams. The tomatoes are sorted into three categories by mass: Small: under 80 g; Medium: 80 g to 104 g; Large: over 104 g.

  • (a) Find, correct to 2 decimal places, the percentage of tomatoes in each of the three categories.
  • (b) Find the value of \(k\) such that \(P(k \leq X < 104) = 0.75\), where \(X\) is the mass of a tomato in grams.

The heights, in metres, of the trees in a forest are normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation 3.6. Given that 75% of the trees are less than 10 m high, find the value of \(\mu\).

Show solution

(a) Find the two "edge" probabilities — small and large — then medium is whatever is left over. (b) and the trees question run the conversion backwards: you're given an area and asked for a value.

(a) Small: under 80 g
\[P(X < 80) = P\left(Z < \frac{80 - 90}{17.7}\right) = P(Z < -0.565) = 0.2860 \;\rightarrow\; 28.60\%\]
(a) Large: over 104 g
\[P(X > 104) = P\left(Z > \frac{104 - 90}{17.7}\right) = P(Z > 0.791) = 0.2145 \;\rightarrow\; 21.45\%\]
(a) Medium: the rest
\[100 - 28.60 - 21.45 = 49.95\%\]
(b) Find k

\(P(X < 104) = 1 - 0.2145 = 0.7855\). The area between \(k\) and 104 is 0.75, so the area to the left of \(k\) is what's left over:

\[P(X < k) = 0.7855 - 0.75 = 0.0355 \;\Longrightarrow\; z = -1.805\] \[k = 90 + (-1.805)(17.7) = 58.05 \text{ g}\]
Trees: find μ

75% of trees are below 10 m, so 10 m is the 75th percentile: \(z = 0.674\).

\[\frac{10 - \mu}{3.6} = 0.674 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu = 10 - 0.674 \times 3.6 = 7.57 \text{ m}\] (a) Small 28.60%, Medium 49.95%, Large 21.45% · (b) k ≈ 58.0 g · Trees: μ ≈ 7.57 m

Example 04 — Finding Both μ and σ

📝 Example 04

The lengths, \(X\) cm, of the leaves of a particular species of tree are normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

  • (a) Find \(P(\mu - \sigma \leq X < \mu + \sigma)\).
  • (b) Find the probability that a randomly selected leaf has a length that is more than 2 standard deviations from the mean.
  • (c) Find the values of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), given that \(P(X < 7.5) = 0.75\) and \(P(X < 8.5) = 0.90\).
Show solution

(a) and (b) don't need actual numbers — "1 standard deviation either side" always becomes \(Z\) between −1 and 1, whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are. (c) gives two clues, each of which turns into one equation. Two equations, two unknowns — solve them together.

(a) Within one standard deviation
\[P(-1 \leq Z < 1) = \Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6827\]
(b) More than two standard deviations away

"More than 2 sd from the mean" means either tail:

\[P(|Z| > 2) = 2\big(1 - \Phi(2)\big) = 2(1 - 0.97725) = 0.0455\]
(c) Two equations

\(P(X < 7.5) = 0.75 \Rightarrow z = 0.6745\), and \(P(X < 8.5) = 0.90 \Rightarrow z = 1.2816\):

\[\frac{7.5 - \mu}{\sigma} = 0.6745 \qquad\qquad \frac{8.5 - \mu}{\sigma} = 1.2816\] \[7.5 = \mu + 0.6745\sigma \qquad\qquad 8.5 = \mu + 1.2816\sigma\]

Subtract the first from the second to eliminate \(\mu\):

\[1 = 0.6071\,\sigma \;\Longrightarrow\; \sigma = 1.647\] \[\mu = 7.5 - 0.6745 \times 1.647 = 6.389\]

Check: \(\frac{8.5 - 6.389}{1.647} = 1.282\) ✓

(a) 0.6827 · (b) 0.0455 · (c) μ ≈ 6.39 cm, σ ≈ 1.65 cm

6. Standard Deviation at Different Levels

Standard deviation indicates how spread out data points are around the mean. A higher standard deviation means the data is more dispersed; a lower one means the data is clustered more closely around the mean.

IntervalPercentage of values
\(\mu \pm 1\sigma\)68.27%
\(\mu \pm 2\sigma\)95.45%
\(\mu \pm 3\sigma\)99.73%

These are the precise versions of the 68–95–99.7 rule from Unit 07.

7. Six Sigma

Definition: Six Sigma is a quality management methodology that aims to improve processes by identifying and removing the causes of defects or errors. The goal is to achieve near perfection in performance.

A normal distribution puts 99.73% of values within \(\pm 3\sigma\). Six Sigma goes further: if a process operates at the Six Sigma level, its variation is so small that the probability of producing a defect is extremely low.

Six Sigma, as stated in the slides
\[99.99966\% \text{ of outputs within } \pm 6\sigma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3.4 \text{ defects per million opportunities (DPMO)}\]
📎 Where 3.4 Per Million Comes From

For exam purposes, quote the slide's figures: 99.99966% and 3.4 DPMO. They are the standard industry numbers. But strictly, a perfectly centred \(\pm 6\sigma\) process would give only about 0.002 defects per million. The 3.4 figure builds in the industry assumption that a process drifts by up to \(1.5\sigma\) over time, so the nearest limit is really \(6 - 1.5 = 4.5\sigma\) away, and \(P(Z > 4.5) \approx 3.4\) per million.
