UNIT 04 · MID TERM

Measures of Dispersion / Variability

1. Why Do We Need Measures of Dispersion?

The measures of location (mean, median, mode) alone do not provide a complete or sufficient description of data.

📝 Motivating Example

Two groups of 6 students with their exam scores:

Group I485052514950
Group II1210099980

The arithmetic mean of each group is 50 — yet the two groups are nothing alike. Group I is tightly clustered; Group II is wildly spread out. Only a measure of dispersion can tell them apart.

Definition: Measures of dispersion (also called measures of variability) are statistical tools used to describe the spread or distribution of a set of data. They indicate how much the data points differ from the central tendency and from each other.
Key Property

A measure of statistical dispersion is a non-negative real number that is zero if all the data are the same, and increases as the data become more diverse.

Understanding dispersion is crucial because it gives insight into the consistency and reliability of the data.

3. Range

Definition: The range is the difference between the largest and the smallest observation.
Range
\[\text{Range} = \text{Largest value} - \text{Smallest value}\]

The greater the spread of the data from the centre of the distribution, the larger the range will be.

⚠️ Limitation

Since the range takes into account only the largest and smallest observations, it is susceptible to considerable distortion if there is one unusual extreme observation.

4. Quartiles and the Interquartile Range (IQR)

Quartiles: Quartiles divide an ordered dataset into four equal parts, each containing 25% of the data. They summarise the distribution and are especially useful for measuring spread and identifying outliers.
  • \(Q_1\) — first quartile (25th percentile), the lower quartile
  • \(Q_2\) — second quartile (50th percentile), the median
  • \(Q_3\) — third quartile (75th percentile), the upper quartile
Interquartile Range (IQR): The IQR measures the spread in the middle 50% of the data — the difference between the observation at \(Q_3\) and the observation at \(Q_1\).
Interquartile Range
\[IQR = Q_3 - Q_1\]
Semi-Interquartile Range (Quartile Deviation)
\[QD = \frac{IQR}{2} = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2} = 0.5 \times IQR\]
Position of the \(k\)th Quartile
\[Q_k = \left(\frac{k(n+1)}{4}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{value}, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3\]
📝 Example 1

Data: 24, 25, 26, 27, 30, 32, 40, 44, 50, 52, 55, 57

  • \(Q_1 = 26.5\)
  • \(Q_2 = \text{Median} = 36\)
  • \(Q_3 = 51\)
\[IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = 51 - 26.5 = 24.5\]
📝 Example 2

Upper quartile \(Q_3 = 69\), lower quartile \(Q_1 = 59\), median \(Q_2 = 65\).

\[IQR = 69 - 59 = 10 \qquad\text{Semi-IQR} = 0.5 \times 10 = 5\]

5. Deciles and Percentiles

Deciles and percentiles are both measures of position, helping to divide a dataset into equal parts for analysis.

Deciles

Definition: Deciles split a dataset into ten equal parts — each decile represents 10% of the data. The nine decile values (\(D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_9\)) serve as cut-off points separating the dataset into ten groups.
  • \(D_1\) (1st decile): the value below which 10% of the data falls
  • \(D_2\) (2nd decile): the value below which 20% of the data falls
  • …
  • \(D_9\) (9th decile): the value below which 90% of the data falls
\(k\)th Decile
\[D_k = \left(\frac{k(n+1)}{10}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{value}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots, 9\]

Percentiles

Definition: Percentiles split a dataset into one hundred equal parts. The 99 percentile values (\(P_1, \ldots, P_{99}\)) each cut off 1% of the data.
\(k\)th Percentile
\[P_k = \left(\frac{k(n+1)}{100}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{value}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots, 99\]
Handy Equivalences

\(Q_1 = P_{25}\)  ·  \(Q_2 = D_5 = P_{50} = \text{Median}\)  ·  \(Q_3 = P_{75}\)  ·  \(D_k = P_{10k}\)

For grouped data, quartiles, deciles and percentiles can also be read off a cumulative frequency curve (ogive): locate the required cumulative frequency on the vertical axis, move across to the curve, then down to the horizontal axis.

6. Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)

Definition: Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) quantifies the average distance between each data point and the mean of the dataset. It provides insight into the dispersion or variability of data.
Mean Absolute Deviation
\[MAD = \frac{\sum |X_i - \bar{X}|}{n}\]
  • \(X_i\) = each data value
  • \(\bar{X}\) = mean of the data
  • \(n\) = number of observations

The absolute value is essential — without it the deviations would always sum to zero, since \(\sum (X_i - \bar{X}) = 0\).

📝 Example

Data: 3, 7, 8, 12, 15 with \(\bar{X} = 9\).

\[MAD = \frac{|3-9| + |7-9| + |8-9| + |12-9| + |15-9|}{5} = \frac{6+2+1+3+6}{5} = \frac{18}{5} = 3.6\]

7. Variance and Standard Deviation

Variance and standard deviation are the fundamental measures of dispersion in statistics, describing how spread out the data is from the mean.

Variance

Definition: Variance measures the mean squared deviation of each data point from the mean.
Population Variance
\[\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \mu)^2}{N}\]
Sample Variance
\[s^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}\]
Why \(n-1\)?

Dividing by \(n-1\) (the degrees of freedom) makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance.

Standard Deviation

Definition: Standard deviation is the square root of the variance and measures the average deviation of data points from the mean. Unlike variance, it is expressed in the same units as the original data.
Population SD
\[\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (X - \mu)^2}{N}}\]
Sample SD
\[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (X - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}}\]

Computational (Short-cut) Form

Expanding the square gives a form that is much faster to compute by hand:

Derivation
\[\begin{aligned} \sigma^2 &= \frac{\sum (X - \mu)^2}{N} \\[4pt] &= \frac{\sum (X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2)}{N} \\[4pt] &= \frac{\sum X^2}{N} - \frac{2\mu \sum X}{N} + \frac{N\mu^2}{N} \\[4pt] &= \frac{\sum X^2}{N} - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 \\[4pt] &= \frac{\sum X^2}{N} - \mu^2 \end{aligned}\]
💡 Exam Shortcut
\[\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum X^2}{N} - \mu^2\]

That is: Variance = mean of squares − square of the mean. Far faster than computing every deviation by hand.

8. Coefficient of Variation (CV)

Definition: The Coefficient of Variation (CV) is a statistical measure that expresses the relative dispersion of a dataset in relation to its mean. Because it is a ratio, it has no units.
Coefficient of Variation
\[CV = \frac{\sigma}{\mu} \times 100\%\]

Interpretation

  • A higher CV indicates greater variability relative to the mean.
  • A lower CV suggests the data points are more consistent around the mean.
💹

Risk vs. Return

Comparing investment risk relative to expected return

📉

Inflation Rates

Comparing volatility across countries or periods

🌍

GDP Growth

Measuring variability of growth across economies

CV is the right tool when comparing the spread of two datasets that have different units or very different means — a raw standard deviation comparison would be misleading.

9. The Five-Number Summary

Definition: The five-number summary is a statistical tool used to describe the distribution of a dataset using five key descriptive statistics.

Minimum value

The smallest data value

First Quartile (Q1)

The median of the lower half — 25th percentile

Median (Q2)

The middle value — 50th percentile

Third Quartile (Q3)

The median of the upper half — 75th percentile

Maximum value

The largest data value

10. Box-and-Whisker Plot (Box Plot)

Definition: A Box-and-Whisker Plot, or simply Box Plot, is a graphical representation of data distribution based on the five-number summary.

It is particularly useful for identifying central tendency, variability, skewness, and outliers in a dataset.

  • The box stretches from \(Q_1\) to \(Q_3\) — its length is the IQR.
  • The line inside the box is the median (\(Q_2\)).
  • The whiskers extend to the smallest and largest values that are not outliers.
  • Points beyond the whiskers are plotted individually as outliers.
Reading Skewness from a Box Plot

If the median sits left of centre with a longer right whisker → positively skewed. If it sits right of centre with a longer left whisker → negatively skewed. A centred median with equal whiskers → symmetric.

11. Skewness

Definition: Skewness measures the asymmetry of a probability distribution. It indicates whether the data is symmetrically distributed or leans more towards one side of the mean.
Type Shape Relationship
Positive skew (right-tailed) Long tail to the right Mean > Median > Mode
No skew (symmetric) Balanced both sides Mean = Median = Mode
Negative skew (left-tailed) Long tail to the left Mean < Median < Mode

Skewness is developed further in Unit 05, where it is expressed using moments.

12. Outliers

Definition: An outlier is a data point that significantly deviates from other values in a dataset. It lies far away from the central tendency (mean, median, or mode) and can influence statistical analyses, leading to misleading conclusions.

Detection of Outliers

Outlier Fences (1.5 × IQR Rule)
\[\text{Lower Bound} = Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR\] \[\text{Upper Bound} = Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR\]

A value \(x\) is an outlier if it lies more than 1.5 times the IQR from the nearest quartile. Thus \(x\) is an outlier if:

\[x < Q_1 - 1.5(Q_3 - Q_1)\] \[x > Q_3 + 1.5(Q_3 - Q_1)\]
📝 Example

If \(Q_1 = 26.5\) and \(Q_3 = 51\), then \(IQR = 24.5\) and \(1.5 \times IQR = 36.75\).

\[\text{Lower} = 26.5 - 36.75 = -10.25 \qquad \text{Upper} = 51 + 36.75 = 87.75\]

Any value below \(-10.25\) or above \(87.75\) is flagged as an outlier.

13. Moments — Introduction

Definition: In statistics, moments are quantitative measures that describe the shape of a distribution. Moments provide information about the central tendency, dispersion, skewness, and kurtosis of a dataset.
Moment about a point \(A\)
\[\mu_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - A)^r\]
Raw Moment — about 0 (the origin)
\[\mu_r' = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i)^r\]
Central Moment — about \(\bar{x}\) (the mean)
\[\mu_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^r\]

The Four Main Types of Moments

Moment Quantity Describes
1st raw momentMeanLocation / central tendency
2nd central momentVarianceDispersion / spread
3rd central momentSkewnessAsymmetry of data
4th central momentKurtosisPeakedness / flatness
Quick Facts

The first central moment is always \(\mu_1 = 0\), and the second central moment is exactly the variance, \(\mu_2 = \sigma^2\).

Moments, skewness and kurtosis are covered in full in Unit 05.
