ESTIMATION · FINAL TERM

Estimation

1. What is Estimation?

Definition: Estimation is the process of inferring or approximating the value of a population parameter based on sample data.

You usually can't measure everyone — every packet a machine fills, or every voter in a country. So you measure a sample and use it to make an educated guess about the whole population. Estimation is the maths of making that guess, and of saying how confident you are in it.

There are two main types:

📍

Point Estimation

A single best-guess value — e.g. "the mean is 81.5".

↔️

Interval Estimation

A range of plausible values — e.g. "the mean is between 78.5 and 84.5, with 98% confidence".

2. Point Estimation

Definition: Point estimation gives a single value as the estimate of an unknown population parameter. For example, the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is often used as a point estimate of the population mean \(\mu\).

Properties of a Good Point Estimator

PropertyMeaningIn plain words
UnbiasednessThe expected value of the estimator equals the true population parameter.On average, it hits the target — no systematic over- or under-shooting.
ConsistencyAs the sample size increases, the estimator becomes more accurate.More data → closer to the truth.
EfficiencyAmong all unbiased estimators, the one with the smallest variance is preferred.Of the fair estimators, pick the least jumpy one.
SufficiencyThe estimator uses all the information in the data relevant to the parameter.It doesn't throw any useful information away.

Unbiased Estimates of the Mean and Variance

Questions often give you only the totals \(\sum x\) and \(\sum x^2\):

Unbiased Estimates
\[\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} \qquad\qquad s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum x^2 - \frac{(\sum x)^2}{n}\right)\]
📎 Supplementary

The \(s^2\) formula isn't in the Estimation handout, but Worksheet 02 needs it (Questions B2 and B3). Dividing by \(n - 1\) rather than \(n\) is what makes it unbiased — the same \(n - 1\) as the sample variance in Unit 04.

3. Interval Estimation — Confidence Intervals

Definition: Interval estimation calculates a range of values, called a confidence interval, within which the population parameter is likely to lie.

Start at your estimate, then step a distance c — the margin of error — down and the same distance up. Everything in between is the confidence interval.

Every Confidence Interval
\[\text{estimate} - c \;<\; \text{parameter} \;<\; \text{estimate} + c \qquad (c = \text{margin of error})\]
CI for a Population Mean
\[\bar{x} - c \;<\; \mu \;<\; \bar{x} + c \qquad\qquad c = z \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

If \(\sigma\) is unknown but \(n\) is large, use the sample's \(s\) in its place: \(c = z \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\).

CI for a Population Proportion
\[\hat{p} - c \;<\; p \;<\; \hat{p} + c \qquad\qquad c = z \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\] \[\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}\]

\(p\) = the population proportion · \(x\) = the number of successes counted in the sample · \(n\) = the total number in the sample.

⚠️ Typo in the Handout

The handout labels both formulas "CI (Confidence Interval population mean)". The second one, with \(\hat{p}\), is the interval for a population proportion.

Finding z — Name It by the Area to Its Left

A confidence level puts that much area in the middle of the curve and splits what's left equally between the two tails. At 90%, that leaves 5% in each tail:

The z-table gives the area to the left of \(z\). That area still includes the left tail — you can't get rid of the initial 5% — so at 90% you look up \(90\% + 5\% = 95\%\). That's why the 90% value is written \(z_{95\%}\).

Confidence levelEach tailLook upz
90%5%\(z_{95\%}\)1.645
95%2.5%\(z_{97.5\%}\)1.960
98%1%\(z_{99\%}\)2.326
99%0.5%\(z_{99.5\%}\)2.576

Think of the interval as a net thrown around your estimate. A wider net (bigger \(z\), so bigger \(c\)) is more likely to catch the true value, so higher confidence means a wider interval. A bigger sample (larger \(n\)) shrinks \(c\), so more data means a tighter net.

Working Backwards

You're givenFind it with
The interval's two limitsThe estimate is the midpoint, and \(c = \dfrac{\text{upper} - \text{lower}}{2}\)
\(c\), want \(\sigma\)\(c = z \times \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma = \dfrac{c\sqrt{n}}{z}\)
\(c\), want \(z\)\(z = c \div \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)  (or \(c \div \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\))
\(z\), want the confidence levelFind the area to the left of \(z\). Each tail \(= 100\% -\) that area, and confidence \(= 100\% - 2 \times\) tail

Applications

  • Estimating the average income of a population from a sample survey.
  • Determining the proportion of voters who support a particular candidate.
  • Estimating the effect of a drug in clinical trials by comparing sample outcomes.

4. Handout Questions

Q1 — Train Journey Time

📝 Q1

The time taken in minutes for a certain daily train journey has a normal distribution with standard deviation 5.8. For a random sample of 20 days the journey times were noted, and the mean journey time was found to be 81.5 minutes.

(a) Calculate a 98% confidence interval for the population mean journey time. [3]

Show solution

We know \(\sigma\), so this is straight substitution: find \(z\) for 98%, work out \(c\), then step \(c\) down and up from \(\bar{x}\).

Given
\[\sigma = 5.8, \qquad n = 20, \qquad \bar{x} = 81.5\]
Find z for 98%

98% in the middle leaves 1% in each tail, so look up 99%:

\[z_{99\%} = 2.326\]
Margin of error c
\[c = z \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 2.326 \times \frac{5.8}{\sqrt{20}} = 3.02\]
The interval
\[\bar{x} - c \;<\; \mu \;<\; \bar{x} + c\] \[81.5 - 3.02 \;<\; \mu \;<\; 81.5 + 3.02\] \[78.48 \;<\; \mu \;<\; 84.52\] 98% CI: 78.48 < μ < 84.52 minutes

Q2 — Finding σ from an Interval

📝 Q2

The widths, \(w\) cm, of a random sample of 150 leaves of a certain kind were measured. The sample mean of \(w\) was found to be 3.12 cm. Using this sample, an approximate 95% confidence interval for the population mean of the widths in centimetres was found to be [3.01, 3.23].

(a) Calculate an estimate of the population standard deviation. [3]

Show solution

This runs the formula backwards. The interval is centred on \(\bar{x} = 3.12\) and reaches \(c = 0.11\) either side. Since \(c = z \times \sigma/\sqrt{n}\), you can solve for \(\sigma\).

Read off c
\[c = 3.23 - 3.12 = 0.11 \qquad\left(\text{half the width: } \tfrac{3.23 - 3.01}{2} = 0.11\right)\]
Find z for 95%

2.5% in each tail, so look up 97.5%: \(z_{97.5\%} = 1.96\).

Solve for σ
\[c = z \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.11 = 1.96 \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{150}}\] \[\sigma = \frac{0.11 \times \sqrt{150}}{1.96} = 0.687\] σ ≈ 0.687 cm

Q3 — Finding the Confidence Level

📝 Q3

A student wishes to estimate the proportion, \(p\), of students at her college who have exactly one brother. She surveys a random sample of 50 students at her college and finds that 18 of them have exactly one brother. She calculates an approximate \(\alpha\)% confidence interval for \(p\) and finds that the lower limit of the confidence interval is 0.244 correct to 3 significant figures.

Find \(\alpha\) correct to the nearest integer. [4]

Show solution

The lower limit is \(\hat{p} - c\), so it gives you \(c\). Then \(c = z \times \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\) gives you \(z\), and \(z\) gives you the confidence level.

Sample proportion
\[\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n} = \frac{18}{50} = 0.36\]
Find c from the lower limit
\[\hat{p} - c = 0.244 \;\Longrightarrow\; c = 0.36 - 0.244 = 0.116\]
Find z
\[c = z \times \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.116 = z \times \sqrt{\frac{0.36 \times 0.64}{50}} = z \times 0.06788\] \[z = \frac{0.116}{0.06788} = 1.709\]
Turn z into a confidence level

From the table, the area to the left of 1.709 is 95.63%. So each tail is \(100\% - 95.63\% = 4.37\%\):

\[\alpha = 100 - 2 \times 4.37 = 91.25\]

Since 0.244 is rounded, the true lower limit lies between 0.2435 and 0.2445. That gives a confidence between 91.1% and 91.4%, so the nearest integer is safely 91.

α = 91
