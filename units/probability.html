Probability

1. What is Probability?

Definition: Probability is the measure of how likely an event is to occur. It quantifies uncertainty.

Range of Probabilities Rule

The probability of an event \(E\) is between 0 and 1, inclusive:

Range Rule
\[0 \leq P(E) \leq 1\]

MeaningImpossibleUnlikelyEven chanceLikelyCertain

2. Basic Terms

🔬

Experiment

An action with an uncertain outcome (e.g. tossing a coin)

🎯

Outcome

A possible result (e.g. getting heads)

🗂️

Sample Space (S)

The set of all possible outcomes

📝 Example

For tossing a coin, \(S = \{H, T\}\).

Theoretical Probability

Based on known possible outcomes:

Theoretical Probability
\[P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}}\]

3. Complement Rule

Complement: \(A'\) is the event that \(A\) does not happen. Between them, \(A\) and \(A'\) cover the whole sample space.
Complement Rule
\[P(A) + P(A') = 1\]
💡 Why It Matters

Rearranged as \(P(A) = 1 - P(A')\), this is the single most useful shortcut in probability. Any question phrased as "at least one" is almost always faster through the complement "none".

4. Addition Rule

Definition: The Addition Rule helps calculate the probability that at least one of two events, or both, occurs.
In Words
\[P(A \text{ or } B \text{ or both}) = P(A \cup B)\]
Addition Rule
\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]
Why Subtract the Overlap?

Adding \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) counts everything in the overlap \(A \cap B\) twice. Subtracting \(P(A \cap B)\) once puts it back to being counted exactly once.

5. Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events

Definition: Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time. (Equivalently: two events are not mutually exclusive if they can happen at the same time.)

If \(A\) and \(B\) are two mutually exclusive events, then there is no overlap at all:

No Overlap
\[P(A \cap B) = 0\]

So the addition rule simplifies:

Addition Rule for Mutually Exclusive Events
\[\therefore\; P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\]
📝 Example

Rolling a die once: "getting a 2" and "getting a 5" are mutually exclusive — one roll cannot be both. But "getting an even number" and "getting a number above 3" are not mutually exclusive, since 4 and 6 satisfy both.

6. Independent Events

Definition: Two events are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the occurrence of the other.

If \(A\) and \(B\) are two independent events, then:

Multiplication Rule for Independent Events
\[P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)\]
📝 Example: Tossing Two Coins

Let \(A\) = getting Heads on the first coin, and \(B\) = getting Heads on the second coin. These events are independent because what you get on the first toss does not influence the second.

\[P(A) = \tfrac12, \quad P(B) = \tfrac12 \;\Rightarrow\; P(A \cap B) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14\]
⚠️ Don't Confuse These Two

Mutually exclusive and independent are not the same thing — they are almost opposites. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together (\(P(A \cap B) = 0\)); independent events often do occur together, just without influencing each other (\(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\)).

7. Dependent Events

Definition: Two events are dependent if the occurrence of one affects the probability of the other.

If \(A\) and \(B\) are dependent, then:

General Multiplication Rule
\[P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)\]

Note that in this case:

\[P(B \mid A) \neq P(B)\]

This is actually the general rule — independence is just the special case where \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\), which collapses it back to \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\).

📝 Example

Drawing two cards from a deck without replacement makes the draws dependent: the first card changes what is left for the second. Drawing with replacement makes them independent.

8. Conditional Probability

Definition: The conditional probability \(P(B \mid A)\) is the probability of \(B\) given that \(A\) has already happened.
Conditional Probability
\[P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \qquad \text{(provided } P(A) > 0\text{)}\]
How to Read It

Conditioning on \(A\) shrinks the sample space to just \(A\). You then ask what fraction of that smaller world also contains \(B\) — hence dividing by \(P(A)\).

The Two Rules of Trees

Multiply along a branch

Follow one path from left to right and multiply the probabilities to get the probability of that complete outcome.

Add down the branches

To find the probability of an event that several paths satisfy, add the end probabilities of those paths.

The probabilities on each set of branches leaving a single node always sum to 1 — a quick way to check a tree before using it.

10. Bayes' Theorem

Definition: Bayes' Theorem is a fundamental concept in probability and statistics that allows us to update the probability of a hypothesis or event based on new evidence or information.

Bayes' Theorem reverses the condition: it allows you to compute \(P(A \mid B)\) from \(P(B \mid A)\).

The Question It Answers

"If an event has already occurred, how does that affect the probability of another event?"

For example: a patient tests positive for a disease — what is the probability that the patient actually has the disease?

Mathematical Formula

For two events \(A\) and \(B\):

Bayes' Theorem
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \times P(A)}{P(B)}\]

Meaning of Each Term

TermMeaningName
\(P(A \mid B)\)Probability of event \(A\) given \(B\)Posterior probability
\(P(B \mid A)\)Probability of event \(B\) given \(A\)Likelihood
\(P(A)\)Initial probability of \(A\)Prior probability
\(P(B)\)Total probability of \(B\)Evidence

Verbal Meaning

Bayes' theorem says:

In Words
\[\text{Updated Probability / Posterior} = \frac{\text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}}{\text{Evidence}}\]
📎 Finding the Evidence P(B)

In practice \(P(B)\) is rarely handed to you — you build it from the branches of a tree. This is the law of total probability: \[P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid A')P(A')\] which is just "add up every path that ends in \(B\)". This step is not spelled out in the handout, but nearly every Bayes question needs it.
